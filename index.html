About me

AI × Biology

machine learning that speaks the language of cells

I work at the intersection of artificial intelligence and biology — building machine-learning methods that learn the language of cells, sequences and molecules.

Most of my research is generative: I design flow-matching, diffusion and probabilistic models that simulate cellular dynamics and predict how biological systems respond to genetic and chemical perturbations. More broadly, I work across sequence- and structure-aware modelling, uncertainty estimation and representation learning — with a consistent goal of turning predictive models into mechanistic, interpretable and experimentally useful insight.

          Photography

          When I'm not teaching machines to read biology, I'm out with a camera learning to read light. Photography is my analogue counterweight — no gradients, no GPUs, just a viewfinder and the patience to wait for a moment to actually arrive.

          I've come to think of it as contemplatio — the old Latin word for looking at something long and hard enough that it changes you. Two inherited habits of seeing sit behind every frame below. The Latin West reads the world in lux et umbra: light and shadow, time and mortality — seize the moment, and remember it will not keep. The contemplative East reads what the West leaves out: the pause, the empty space, the quiet beauty of things precisely because they pass. I shoot mostly on Fujifilm glass, somewhere between documentary and daydream, and I've paired each photograph here with the thought I was actually chasing when the shutter fell — in Latin, Japanese, Chinese, Korean or Sanskrit. Not decoration. Directions for looking.

          Lux et Umbra · the Latin eye

          Carpe diem seize the day

          Memento mori remember you die

          Festina lente make haste, slowly

          To the Stoics, seeing clearly was a moral discipline. A Roman frame is built from light and its loss — the sunbeam, the long shadow, the fact that the hour will not come again. You attend to the world because it is leaving.

          無常 Mujō · the Eastern eye

          道 Dào the Way that cannot be named

          一期一会 Ichi-go ichi-e one time, one meeting

          間 Ma the charged interval, the pause

          From Kyoto to Seoul, the contemplative traditions prize what a Western frame crops out — the emptiness that gives the subject room, the impermanence that makes a moment precious. Nothing lasts; that is not the tragedy but the point.

          Into the Green

          Arashiyama, Kyoto · Japan

          Dawn in the grove, before the tour buses — the one hour it is ever empty. The path bends and disappears; you can't see where it leads, so you just walk. That is the whole of Taoism in a gravel track. Overhead, the sun breaks through the canes in scattered coins of light — the Japanese keep a word for exactly this and nothing else: komorebi (木漏れ日).

          道 · Michi / Dào the Way — Taoism & Zen

          道可道，非常道 — "The Way that can be spoken is not the eternal Way." — Laozi, Tao Te Ching, ch. 1

          02 / Featured

          Twelve Wingbeats a Second

          Farne Islands · England

          One puffin already home on the ledge; one still coming in hot, orange feet out like landing gear. They beat those tiny wings some 400 times a minute — roughly the number of frames I burned to freeze a single one. But you can't hurry a bird into the right place; you hold the lens still and let it arrive. Augustus called that festina lente. The Zen archers had their own name for the shot you take without deciding to: mushin (無心), no-mind.

          Festina lente make haste slowly — Augustus, via Suetonius

          "Festina lente" — hasten slowly. Suetonius records it as the emperor Augustus' favourite maxim; he hurried at nothing.

          03 / Featured

          Break

          Ligurian Sea · Italy

          Three hours of flat grey water, then one full second airborne — the whole animal clear of the sea, spray still hanging off its tail. The Greeks split time in two: chronos, the grey hours that just tick by, and kairós, the one instant that opens and asks to be taken. The Japanese archers name what gets you through the wait — zanshin (残心), the readiness that never drops even when nothing is happening. And when it happens, Horace has the only sane advice: pluck the day.

          Carpe diem pluck the day — Horace, Odes I.11

          "Carpe diem, quam minimum credula postero." — Horace — "Pluck the day, trusting the next as little as you can."

          04 / Featured

          Long Shadows in Rome

          Castel Sant'Angelo, Rome · Italy

          No building says it plainer. Castel Sant'Angelo was raised as the mausoleum of the emperor Hadrian — a tomb — and here it is, floodlit gold under a cold sky, the bronze archangel still sheathing his sword on the roof. A slow shutter smears the passers-by into umbrae — the Latin word means both "shadow" and "ghost of the dead." Seen against the stone (sub specie aeternitatis, as Spinoza would later put it), it is the still figures who turn out to be fleeting. In a Roman triumph a slave rode behind the general whispering the oldest version of the thought: respice post te; hominem te memento — look behind you, and remember you are only a man.

          Memento mori remember you must die — with lux et umbra

          "Omnia, Lucili, aliena sunt, tempus tantum nostrum est." — Seneca, Epistulae 1 — "Everything is on loan, Lucilius; time alone is ours."

          05 / Featured

          Golden Hour at Gwanghwamun

          Seoul · South Korea

          Seoul still sets stone guardians at its gates — haetae, the old mythical keepers — and this one sat dead still while a red bus, a stranger and the whole restless city slid past behind it. Stopped down to f/8, the low sun broke into a clean star right at its chest. Two ideas met in one frame: the Korean painter's yeobaek (여백), the deliberate empty space that lets a subject breathe; and Laozi's instruction to the sage — soften the light, merge with the dust — which is, almost word for word, what a backlit guardian does at sunset. To stand before such a figure and really look is what Sanskrit calls darśana: beholding, and being beheld in return.

          여백 · Yeobaek the charged empty space — Korean aesthetics

          和其光，同其塵 — "Soften the glare, merge with the dust." — Laozi, Tao Te Ching, ch. 56

          Seize the day — because you meet it only once.

          East and West reach the same doorway from opposite sides. Carpe diem and 一期一会; memento mori and 無常 — one tradition urges you to grip the fleeting moment, the other to let it fall open. A camera does both at once: it keeps the instant and admits, in the same click, that the instant is already gone. That contradiction is the whole of the art. Thank you for looking slowly.
